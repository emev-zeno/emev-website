Fix It First

Why more than half of financial complaints shouldn’t need an ombudsman.

60,422 of 117,002

complaints to the Australian Financial Complaints Authority (AFCA) in 2025–26 closed at its first stage, registration and referral. That is 52%.

At that stage AFCA doesn’t investigate. It sends the complaint back to the bank, insurer or super fund, and the firm sorts it out. So in more than half of cases, the firm was able to fix the problem. It just didn’t fix it before the customer gave up and went to the ombudsman.

John’s note

ASIC’s data changes the question. Firms resolve most complaints within a day, so the problem isn’t that they can’t resolve complaints. A smaller group slips through: complaints nobody recognised as complaints, scam cases sent to the wrong team, and responses that don’t explain the decision. Those are the cases the future state targets.

3

Problem statement

Customers are taking complaints to AFCA that their bank, insurer or fund could have resolved itself. In 2025–26, 60,422 complaints closed at AFCA’s first stage, after the firm got the complaint back and fixed it. That costs customers time and stress. It costs firms AFCA fees, staff time and trust. And it uses ombudsman capacity that complex cases need.

Who is affected

  • Customers, who have to complain twice. Scam victims and people in hardship are hit hardest.
  • Complaints teams at banks, insurers and super funds, who handle the same complaint at two stages.
  • AFCA, which registers and refers cases that never needed an ombudsman.
  • Product owners, who don’t see why the complaint happened in the first place.

Impact

  • 51.6% of complaints received were closed at registration and referral.
  • Banks account for 44,350 complaints received, and general insurers 26,906. The volume sits with a few firm types.
  • Deposit taking and payments is the largest progressed product group (17.6%), the area where scam losses show up.
  • Complaint volumes rose from about 8,300 in December to about 10,800 in May.

Root-cause hypotheses

Where published evidence exists, it’s noted. The rest need a firm’s own IDR data.

  • H1 Complaints aren’t recognised as complaints at first contact, so the IDR clock never starts. Evidence: ASIC found general insurers failed to identify 1 in 6 complaints (REP 802).
  • H2 Frontline staff can’t offer a remedy, so customers wait for a specialist who calls back late.
  • H3 Scam and hardship cases aren’t flagged early, so they reach the wrong team. Evidence: 67% of scam complaints to AFCA in 2023–24 were resolved once referred back to the firm.
  • H4 IDR responses are generic and don’t explain the decision, so customers escalate to get one. Evidence: 1 in 8 IDR responses to rejected complaints missed required content (REP 802).
  • H5 Firms can’t see the warning signs (repeat contact, AFCA mentioned, deadline near) until it’s too late.
John’s note

I wrote the hypotheses before looking for evidence, then checked each one against what ASIC and AFCA have published. Three have public support. The other two I would test with a firm’s own complaint records before building anything.

Current-state process

How a complaint moves today, from the customer to a firm’s internal dispute resolution (IDR) and then to AFCA. Red markers show pain points.

Scroll sideways to see the whole map.

  1. 1Not logged as a complaint. A call about a missing transfer gets treated as a service request, so no one owns it and the IDR clock never starts.
  2. 2Slow or generic IDR response. The customer waits up to 30 days and gets a letter that doesn’t explain the decision or what happens next.
  3. 3Customer escalates to get attention. Going to AFCA becomes the way to reach someone with authority at the firm.
  4. 4Fixed only after AFCA is involved. In 60,422 cases the firm resolved it at referral. The fix was possible earlier, and now there’s an AFCA case on record.
  5. 5Complex cases queue behind avoidable ones. Scam and hardship cases that need investigation share capacity with cases the firm could have closed.

I built this map from AFCA’s and ASIC’s public descriptions of the process. At a real firm I would walk it through with the IDR team in a workshop, because the map on paper is rarely how the work actually happens.

Requirements

Four epics for the triage capability, with user stories and acceptance criteria. Select an epic to expand it.

EPIC 1Recognise and classify every complaint

US-1.1 · Flag complaint signals

As a customer service officer, I want the system to flag when a customer contact contains complaint signals, so that I log it as a complaint and the IDR clock starts.

Given a contact note that includes dissatisfaction words (for example “unhappy”, “not good enough”, “want this fixed”)

When the officer saves the note

Then a “Possible complaint” prompt shows, and the officer must choose “Log complaint” or “Not a complaint” with a reason.

US-1.2 · Classify product and issue

As a complaints officer, I want each complaint pre-classified by product and issue using AFCA’s product groups, so that it reaches the right team without me reading it first.

Given a logged complaint with free-text description

When triage runs

Then a product group and issue are suggested with a confidence score and the words that drove it. The officer can accept or change them, and changes are recorded to retrain the model.

EPIC 2Spot likely scams early

US-2.1 · Scam flag

As a scam specialist, I want complaints that describe common scam patterns flagged at intake, so that I can act while recovery is still possible.

Given a complaint mentioning a pattern such as “safe account”, “pretending to be the bank” or “remote access”

When triage runs

Then the complaint is flagged “Likely scam”, the patterns found are listed, and it is routed to the scam queue.

US-2.2 · Scams Prevention Framework record

As a compliance manager, I want scam complaints to carry the fields we will need under the Scams Prevention Framework, so that we’re ready when consumers can bring scam complaints to AFCA from 31 March 2027.

Given a complaint flagged as a likely scam

When the case owner opens it

Then they must record scam type, payment channel, amount lost and date reported before the case can close.

EPIC 3Score escalation risk and route by it

US-3.1 · Explainable risk score

As a team leader, I want each complaint scored low, medium or high escalation risk with the reasons shown, so that the team works the riskiest cases first and can explain why.

Given a complaint that mentions AFCA or the ombudsman and repeat contact

When triage runs

Then the risk is at least “Medium”, each contributing reason is listed, and the queue sorts high before medium before low.

US-3.2 · Named owner for medium and high

As a customer, I want a named person to call me soon after I complain, so that I don’t have to chase.

Given a complaint scored medium or high

When it is logged

Then it is assigned to a named owner and a call task is due within 1 business day. If the task is overdue the team leader is alerted.

EPIC 4Meet IDR deadlines and learn from them

US-4.1 · Deadline by complaint type

As a complaints officer, I want the response deadline calculated from the date received and the complaint type, so that I never miss an IDR timeframe.

Given a complaint received on a date, about a default notice while the customer is in hardship

When triage runs

Then the deadline is set to 21 calendar days after receipt, not 30, and the rule used is shown.

US-4.2 · Root-cause reporting

As a product owner, I want a monthly view of complaints by product, issue and outcome, so that I can fix the cause and not just the case.

Given closed complaints for the month

When the report runs

Then it shows counts by product and issue, the share resolved at first contact, and the share that later reached AFCA.

8

Business case

Three options for a mid-sized bank. All costs are indicative assumptions for comparison, not quotes, and would be confirmed in a detailed estimate.

Option A

Do nothing

Indicative cost
$0 up front. Ongoing cost of AFCA fees and double handling continues.
Benefits
No change effort.
Risks
Scam complaint volumes likely to rise once consumers can take scam complaints to AFCA under the Scams Prevention Framework from 31 March 2027. Regulator attention on IDR performance.
Option B

Process and training uplift

Indicative cost
$250k–$400k one-off (process redesign, training, remedy limits). About $100k a year to sustain.
Benefits
Better complaint recognition and faster frontline remedies. Quick to start.
Risks
Depends on staff memory and consistency. Hard to measure. Gains fade with turnover. No early scam flag.
Option C · Recommended

AI-assisted triage tool

Indicative cost
$600k–$1.1M one-off (build or configure, integration, testing). $150k–$250k a year to run and tune.
Benefits
Every complaint classified, risk-scored and deadlined at intake. Scam cases routed on day one. Data to find root causes.
Risks
Misclassification, staff over-trusting the tool, integration with legacy CRM. Mitigated by an explainable model, human sign-off and a pilot.

Recommendation

Go with Option C, delivered in phases, with Option B’s process changes as phase one. The tool only works if staff can act on what it tells them, so remedy limits and training come first. Pilot the triage on one product group, deposit taking and payments, the largest share of progressed complaints (17.6%) and the main scam channel. Expand after 3 months if the pilot meets its measures.

Measures of success (baselined from the firm’s own IDR data before the pilot): share of complaints resolved at first contact; median days to IDR response; share of IDR complaints that later reach AFCA; scam complaints routed to the scam team within 1 business day.

How to size the benefit: for every complaint the firm resolves inside IDR instead of at AFCA referral, it avoids the AFCA case fee and a second round of handling. Use the firm’s own AFCA fee schedule and referral volumes. No benefit figure is claimed here because the Datacube doesn’t publish per-firm costs.

John’s note

I didn’t put a dollar benefit on the recommended option. The public data doesn’t support one, and a made-up return on investment would weaken everything else in the case. The firm’s own fee and volume data would set that number.

Try the triage prototype

Pick a sample or write your own complaint. A small machine-learning model classifies it in your browser and shows which words drove each answer. Nothing is sent anywhere.

Results will appear here.

How it works

  1. Classify. A naive Bayes model learns how often each word and word pair appears in each product and issue category, from 60 labelled example complaints. For new text it compares those likelihoods and gives a score for every category.
  2. Flag scams. A second naive Bayes model, trained on the same examples, scores how scam-like the text is.
  3. Score escalation risk. A points scorecard adds up signals such as mentioning AFCA, repeat contact, vulnerability and the amount lost. In production the weights would be fitted with logistic regression to past complaints that did, or didn’t, go on to AFCA.
  4. Set the deadline. ASIC RG 271 timeframes: 30 calendar days, 45 for superannuation trustees, 21 for complaints about a default notice or hardship request.

Responsible AI

  • Explainable. Words that pushed the result are highlighted in the complaint, and every risk point is listed.
  • Human in the loop. The model suggests. A complaints officer confirms the category, the scam flag and any remedy.
  • Fails safe. Below 55% confidence the complaint is routed to manual review.
  • No customer data stored. The text never leaves your browser.
  • Honest about limits. The examples were written for this demo. A real model would be trained on the firm’s own labelled complaints, monitored for accuracy by product, and tested for bias across customer groups.
See the 60 training examples
John’s note

I chose naive Bayes over a large language model on purpose. It runs in the browser in milliseconds, it can be retrained by the complaints team, and every result can be traced back to the words that caused it. In a regulated complaints process, that traceability matters more than fluency. An LLM would be the right tool for drafting the IDR response, with the same human sign-off, but not for deciding where a complaint goes.

Method and sources

  • Data: AFCA Datacube, data.afca.org.au. All members, 1 July 2025 to 30 June 2026. Annual totals taken as published. Monthly values read from the Datacube chart and labelled approximate.
  • Analysis: stage outcomes, firm type and product group compared, then checked for reconciliation (see data quality).
  • Regulation: ASIC Regulatory Guide 271, Internal dispute resolution, for IDR timeframes. Scams Prevention Framework for the 31 March 2027 date from which consumers can bring scam complaints to AFCA.
  • Illustrative content: personas, journeys, costs and process timings are assumptions to show the method. They are labelled where they appear.
  • Wider picture: AFCA media releases of 17 Oct 2023, 1 Aug 2024, 31 Oct 2024, 23 Jul 2025 and 4 Aug 2026 (afca.org.au/news/media-releases). ASIC media releases 24-264MR (REP 801) and 24-268MR (REP 802), asic.gov.au. Preliminary figures are marked where used.
  • Prototype: multinomial naive Bayes with Laplace smoothing, trained in the browser on 60 example complaints written for this demo. Accuracy is measured with leave-one-out cross-validation on those examples, which shows the method works but says nothing about real-world accuracy. Plain JavaScript, no external services.
